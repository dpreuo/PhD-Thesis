
\section{Kitaev's Honeycomb Model} \label{sec:honeycomb_model}

Let us begin by providing a review of the model as it was originally proposed by Kitaev in~\cite{kitaev_anyons_2006}. We shall present the spin Hamiltonian, as well as the Majorana transformation used to diagonalise it. Following this, we shall discuss the ground-state phase diagram and finally the presence of anyonic excitations. In \textsection\ref{sec:amorphous_kitaev_model}, we will describe the extension of this model to arbitrary and amorphous lattice geometry. Thus, here we will focus primarily on the aspects of the Honeycomb model that are relevant for the amorphous case.

The Kitaev honeycomb model is defined for a set of spin half degrees of freedom on the vertices of a honeycomb lattice, as shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:honeycomb_kitaev}a. Note that this lattice is bipartite, so the sites can be organised into two sublattices A and B, where bonds are always between two sites in different sublattices. The bonds in this lattice are labelled with an index $\alpha \in \{x, y, z\}$, such that each site connects to one bond of each type. On each bond, neighbouring spins interact with a bond-directional Ising-type coupling. The Hamiltonian is given by
\begin{align} \label{eqn:kitaev_original_hamiltonian}
    H = -J^x \sum_{x\textup{-bonds}} \sigma_j^x \sigma_k^x
    -J^y \sum_{y\textup{-bonds}} \sigma_j^y \sigma_k^y
    -J^z \sum_{z\textup{-bonds}} \sigma_j^z \sigma_k^z,
\end{align}
where the parameters $J^\alpha$ determine the strength of the $x$, $y$ and $z$ bonds, and $\sigma_j^\alpha$ represents a Pauli operator acting on the spin at site $j$. The exact solution of this model -- as with all integrable systems --  depends on the presence of an extensive set of conserved quantities, given by the product of the bond operators around a given hexagonal plaquette, $p$, 
\begin{align}\label{eqn:wp_definition}
    W_p = \prod_{\braket{jk} \in p}
    \sigma_j^\alpha \sigma_k^\alpha,
\end{align}
where the order of the $\sigma_j$ operators is arranged such that they run clockwise around the hexagonal plaquette. Using that standard Pauli multiplication rules, we can show that the product can be simplified into a product of the six Pauli operators that connect outwards from each site on the hexagon. For example, the plaquette shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:honeycomb_kitaev}b has $W_p$ given by
\begin{align} \label{eqn:wp_operator}
    W_p = \sigma^z_1 \sigma^x_2 \sigma^y_3
    \sigma^z_4 \sigma^x_5 \sigma^y_6.
\end{align} 
All $W_p$ operators commute with each other and $H$. Furthermore, we have $W_p^2 = 1$, which means that $W_p$ have eigenvalues $\pm 1$. The Hilbert space has dimension $2^N$, where $N$ is the number of spins. Since we have $N/2$ plaquettes in the system, these conserved quantities allow us to split the Hilbert space into $2^{N/2}$ sectors according to the eigenvalues of $\{W_p\}$.

\subsection{Spins to Fermions to Majoranas} \label{sec:spins_to_majoranas}

Although the Kitaev model can be solved with a Jordan-Wigner transformation~\cite{feng_topological_2007,chen_exact_2007,chen_exact_2008,nasu_majorana_2023}, here we will follow the original construction in terms of Majorana fermions. The first step is an old trick, often referred to as the parton construction~\cite{senthil_z_2_2000, wen_theory_1996, wen_quantum_2007}. We introduce a pair of fermion operators for each site, $f^\dag_\uparrow$ and $f^\dag_\downarrow$, which create a spin up and spin down state respectively. Spin operators are straightforwardly modified to
\begin{align}
    \sigma^\alpha \rightarrow f^\dag _{\mu} \sigma^\alpha_{\mu \nu} f_\nu.
\end{align}
In performing this transformation we have artificially enlarged our Hilbert space, since each site can now host four states. Two of these states are the original spin up and down states, constituting the physical part of the enlarged Hilbert space, $\mathcal H_{\textup{phys}}$. The other two correspond to no spin at all, or both a spin up and spin down on a single site. These states contribute an unphysical auxiliary Hilbert space $\mathcal H_{\textup{aux}}$.
\begin{center}
    \includegraphics[scale=1.1]{kitaev_model/figures/hilbert_space_fermions.pdf}
\end{center}
We construct a parity operator that determines whether a state is in the physical or auxiliary Hilbert space using a product over fermionic number operators on a single site, $ n_{j\mu} = f^\dag_{j \mu} f_{j \nu}$,
\begin{align}
    D_{j} = -(1 - 2 n_{j\uparrow})(1 - 2 n_{j\downarrow}).
\end{align}
This operator has the property that $D_j \ket \psi = + (-)\ket \psi$ for states in the physical (auxiliary) Hilbert space. Next let us decompose each fermion into a pair of Majorana fermions according to the following substitution,
\begin{align}
    f_\downarrow &= \frac{1}{2} (c + i b^z), \\ 
    f_\uparrow &= \frac{1}{2} (b^y + i b^x),
\end{align}
where the Majorana operators have standard Majorana commutation relations,
\begin{align}
    c^\dag = c,\qquad \{c_j , c_k \} = 2\delta_{jk},
\end{align}
with the same relations for $b$-type Majoranas. In this new formalism, a fermionic parity operator becomes
\begin{align}
    i c b^z = 2 f_\downarrow ^\dag f_\downarrow - 1,
\end{align}
with the corresponding identity for $f_\uparrow$ using $b^y$ and $b^x$. Thus, the parity operator $D$ becomes
\begin{align}
    D =  b^x b^y b^z c.
\end{align}

In the Majorana representation, the spin operators take the following form,
\begin{align}
    \sigma^x  & \rightarrow \frac{i}{2} (c b^x - b^z b^y), \\ 
    \sigma^y  & \rightarrow \frac{i}{2} (c b^y + b^z b^x), \\ 
    \sigma^z  & \rightarrow \frac{i}{2} (c b^z - b^y b^x).
\end{align}
This expression can be simplified by requiring that we act only on states in the physical subspace -- which satisfy $D \ket \psi = \ket \psi$. First we note that the spin operators preserve the physical subspace since $D$ commutes with any product of two Majoranas on the same site, e.g.~$[cb^x, D] = 0$. Any state in the physical Hilbert space satisfies $c b^z b^y b^x \ket \psi = \ket \psi$, which can rearranged into the following identity,
\begin{align}
    c b^z \ket \psi &= - b^y b^x \ket \psi,
\end{align}
with an equivalent identity for any other pairing of the Majoranas. Thus, we see that when acted {\it only on the physical subspace} the spin operators may be written as
\begin{align} \label{sigma_to_majorana}
    \sigma^x = i c b^x,\qquad  \sigma^y = i c b^y ,\qquad  \sigma^z = i c b^z.
\end{align}

    \begin{center}
        \includegraphics[]{kitaev_model/figures/spin_to_majorana.pdf}
        \caption[A depiction of the three steps in transforming the spin Hamiltonian into a quadratic Majorana Hamiltonian.]{
            A depiction of the three steps in transforming the spin Hamiltonian into a quadratic Majorana Hamiltonian.
            \textbf{(a)}~A single $z$-bond with two spins located at the endpoints, labelled with $j$ and $k$.
            \textbf{(b)}~After the transformation to the Majorana basis each spin becomes a set of four Majorana fermions. On each site, the $b^x$, $b^y$ and $b^z$ Majorana takes part only in the $x$, $y$ and $z$ bond respectively. The $c$-Majorana takes part in all three couplings. 
            \textbf{(c)}~Once the eigenvalues of the $u_{jk}$ operators have been assigned, we are left with a quadratic Hamiltonian on the $c_j$ degrees of freedom only, with hopping determined by the $u_{jk}$ values, where the arrow indicates the direction in which $u_{jk} = +1$.
        }\label{fig:spin_to_maj}
    \end{center}
\end{figure}%
We are now in a position to transform the Hamiltonian into the Majorana basis. Applying the identities derived above and reordering the Majorana operators, we arrive at the following,
\begin{align} \label{eqn:kitaev_ham_majorana}
    H = -\sum_{jk} J^\alpha_{jk} 
    \sigma_j^\alpha \sigma_k^\alpha \rightarrow 
    \sum_{jk} J^\alpha_{jk}
    (i b^\alpha_k b^\alpha_j) (i c_j c_k ).
\end{align}
Note that the Majoranas forming $\hat u_{jk} = i b^\alpha_k b^\alpha_j$ appear only once in this Hamiltonian. This means that all $\hat u$ operators satisfy $[\hat u_{jk},\hat u_{mn}] = 0$ and $[\hat u_{jk},H] = 0$. Furthermore, they satisfy $\hat u_{jk}^2 = \1$, ensuring that their eigenvalues are $\pm 1$. Thus, these represent an extensive set of conserved quantities, where we may partition the Majorana Hilbert space according to the eigenvalues of these operators, $u_{jk}=\pm 1$. For a given choice of bond variables, Eqn.~\ref{eqn:kitaev_ham_majorana} reduces to a quadratic Majorana Hamiltonian
\begin{equation}\label{eqn:majorana_hamiltonian_with_u}
    H_u = \frac{i}{4}\sum_{ j,k }A_{jk} c_j c_k,
\end{equation}
where 
\begin{align}\label{eqn:iA_definition}
    A_{jk}=2J^{\alpha}u_{jk}.
\end{align} 
Note that the bond variables satisfy $u_{jk} = -u_{kj}$. Thus, the matrix $A$ is real and antisymmetric. We may graphically represent the bond variables by replacing every edge in the lattice with an arrow, where the arrow depicts the direction in which $u_{jk}$ is positive. 

Once a set of $\{u_{jk}\}$ have been chosen, the Hamiltonian $H_u$ can be solved to find the ground state $\ket{\psi_u}$. In general one might expect each distinct set of $\{u_{jk}\}$ to correspond to a unique system and thus a unique ground state, however as we shall see in the next section, the $u$ terms may be understood as a $\mathbb Z_2$ gauge field, where the only physical degrees of freedom are the $\mathbb Z_2$ fluxes through each plaquette. 

\subsection{Gauge Transformations and Fluxes}

\begin{figure} 
    \begin{center}
        \includegraphics[]{kitaev_model/figures/gauge_transformation.pdf}
        \caption[A depiction of a gauge transformation.]{
            A depiction of a gauge transformation. We start with the configuration of $\{u_{jk}\}$ shown on the left. Now we apply a gauge transformation to the system by applying $D_j$ operators to all sites in some subset $\mathcal S$, here coloured red. All bonds that connect to one red site have the sign of $u$ flipped. Bonds between two red sites are flipped twice, and so remain invariant. The flipped bonds are highlighted. This is pictorially represented by the pink loops that encircle all the red sites. Any bond that crosses a pink loop has $u_{jk}$ flipped. 
        }\label{fig:gauge_transformation}
    \end{center}
\end{figure}%
Let us consider the effect of the parity operators $D_j$ on our system. Before a set of $u_{jk}$ values are chosen, the Hamiltonian in Eqn.~\ref{eqn:kitaev_ham_majorana} satisfies $[D_j , H] = 0$ for $\forall j$. However, once $u_{jk}$ are no longer operators, $D_j$ has the effect of flipping the sign of all $u$ terms that connect to site $j$, since
\begin{align}
    D_j u_{jk} c_j c_k D_j = -u_{jk} c_j c_k .
\end{align}
Thus, the effect applying a set of $D_j$ on some subset of sites ($j \in \mathcal S$) is to transform to a new set of bond operators $\{u\} \rightarrow \{u'\}$, where every bond that touches one flipped site has $u'_{jk} = - u_{jk}$. Pictorially, this can be represented as flipping all bond operators on the boundary between sites in the subset $\mathcal S$ and those not included. This is shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:gauge_transformation}. The transformation effected by $D_j$ has no effect on the spectrum of $H_u$, thus we may interpret $D_j$ as a gauge transformation, transporting us to a different set of $\{u_{jk}\}$ that represents the same physical system.

\begin{shaded} 
    {\it Proof that $D_j$ leaves the spectrum invariant --- }
    This can be seen by examining how the $A$ matrix is changed in Eqn.~\ref{eqn:kitaev_ham_majorana}. A single $D_j$ operator's effect on $A$ can be represented as
    \begin{align}
        A \rightarrow R A R,
    \end{align}
    where R is a diagonal matrix given by
    \begin{align}
        R_{ii} = \begin{cases}
            +1 \textup{ for } i \neq j, \\
            -1\textup{ for } i = j.
        \end{cases}
    \end{align}
    Clearly, $R$ is a unitary matrix, so the spectrum of $A$ is unaffected. Thus, the spectrum of $H_u$ is the same as the spectrum of $H_{u'}$ 
\end{shaded}

Since $H_u$ is not invariant under the action of $D_j$, the ground state $\ket{\psi_u}$ does not live in the physical Hilbert space. To construct a physical state we must apply a projector onto $\mathcal{H}_{\textup{phys}}$, given by
\begin{align}
    P = \prod_j \frac{1 + D_j}2.
\end{align}
From the form of $P$, we see that the physical state $\ket{\psi_{\textup{phys}}} = P \ket{\psi_u}$ is found by taking an equal superposition of every possible gauge-equivalent arrangement of~$\{u_{jk}\}$.

The above argument does not imply that {\it all} sets of $u_{jk}$ are equivalent. To see why, let us construct a gauge-invariant quantity -- the $\mathbb Z_2$ flux through each plaquette $p$,
\begin{equation} \label{eqn:z2_flux_def}
    \phi_p = \prod_{(j,k) \in p} -iu_{jk},
\end{equation}
where the product is taken over the $u_{jk}$ values going clockwise around each plaquette. The gauge invariance of this can be seen clearly from Fig.~\ref{fig:gauge_transformation}. A gauge transformation always corresponds to flipping over closed loops (depicted with a pink line). Each individual plaquette will always have an even number of bonds flipped by such a transformation, since every loop must both enter and exit the plaquette, leaving the value of $\phi_p$ invariant. In fact, the plaquette flux defined here is identical to the conserved quantity defined in Eqn.~\ref{eqn:wp_definition} which can be seen by applying the substitution in Eqn.~\ref{sigma_to_majorana} to the definition of $W_p$. Thus, gauge inequivalent sectors of the Hilbert space may be labelled by the values of $\phi_p = \pm 1$ through each plaquette. In order to change the flux through a given plaquette, we perform a line of flips that terminates at the plaquette, as shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:flux_flips}a. Thus, in periodic boundary conditions, it is only possible to flip $\phi_p$ in pairs, however in an open or infinite system we may flip a single flux with a line that starts at that plaquette and extends to the boundary of the system, or to infinity. 

Finally, we note that in periodic boundaries every flux sector is fourfold degenerate. This is because one may flip a line of $u_{jk}$ that wraps the system in either the $x$ or $y$-direction, producing a new flux sector that has identical values of $\phi_p$, but is not gauge equivalent to the starting state. To measure this, we may construct a pair of global fluxes $\Phi_x$ and $\Phi_y$, by taking a product of bond operators around a loop $L_x$ or $L_y$ that wraps the system in the $x$ or $y$ direction
\begin{equation} \label{eqn:non_contractible_flux}
    \Phi_\alpha = \prod_{(j,k) \in L_\alpha} -iu_{jk}.
\end{equation}

\subsection{Time Reversal Symmetry}\label{sec:hex_trs}

Here, we add a brief comment about the effect of time reversal symmetry (TRS) in our system. In the original spin-formulation, time reversal is performed by applying the following anti-unitary operator to each spin,
\begin{align}
    \hat T = \mathcal K (-i\sigma^y), 
\end{align}
where $\mathcal K$ denotes complex conjugation. 
This operator satisfies
\begin{align}
    \hat T \sigma^\alpha_j \hat T^{-1} = - \sigma^\alpha_j.
\end{align}
Thus, the Hamiltonian (Eqn.~\ref{eqn:kitaev_original_hamiltonian}) is manifestly TRS invariant, since every term contains two spin operators. In the Majorana basis, time reversal is defined as
\begin{align}
    \hat T = \mathcal K,
\end{align}
and does not change any of the Majorana operators,
\begin{align}
    \hat T c_j \hat T^{-1} = c_j, \qquad \hat T b_j \hat T^{-1} = b_j.
\end{align}
This means that the Majorana Hamiltonian is not obviously TRS invariant, since the time reversal operator has the property
\begin{align}
    \hat T iu_{jk}c_jc_k \hat T^{-1} = -iu_{jk}c_jc_k,
\end{align}
so has the effect of flipping all bond operators in the system, $u_{jk} \rightarrow -u_{jk}$. On a bipartite system such as the Honeycomb this has no effect, since we can flip all bonds in the system by applying a gauge flip operator $D_j$ to every spin on the $A$ sublattice. The time reversed Hamiltonian is gauge equivalent to the original Hamiltonian. In general, provided the Majorana Hamiltonian has no coupling terms between two sites on the same sublattice, we are guaranteed that a gauge transformation exists that can map the time reversed state back, so time reversal has no effect on the system. 

\subsection{Solving the Hamiltonian}

Once a flux sector has been chosen, we are left with a Majorana Hamiltonian, $H_u$, as defined in Eqn.~\ref{eqn:majorana_hamiltonian_with_u}. The full methodology for diagonalising such a Hamiltonian is detailed in Appendix \ref{sec:majorana_diagonalisation} -- we present only the broad strokes here. We diagonalise the Hamiltonian by rotating to a new Majorana basis consisting of pairs of operators $\tilde c_j', \tilde c_j''$, 
\begin{align}
    \tilde c_j = O_{jk}c_k,
\end{align}
where $O$ is a real orthogonal matrix. In this basis the Hamiltonian takes the form 
\begin{align}
    H = \sum_{ j } \varepsilon_j\, i \tilde c_j' \tilde c_j''.
\end{align}
Each of these paired sets of Majorana operators may now be transformed back to a new fermionic basis according to the identity $ i \tilde c_j' \tilde c_j'' = 2 f_i^\dag f_i - 1$. Thus, the Hamiltonian becomes
\begin{align}
    H = \sum_{ j } \varepsilon_j\,(2 f_i^\dag f_i - 1).
\end{align}
The ground state energy can now be read directly from the Hamiltonian, since it corresponds to leaving every fermionic state unoccupied. 
\begin{align}
    \egs = -\sum_j \varepsilon_j.
\end{align}
For each eigen-energy, there exists a fermionic excitation, created by applying the corresponding $f^\dag_j$ to the ground state, with energy cost $2\varepsilon_j$. 

In practice, it is often unnecessary to find the real orthogonal matrix $O$, and corresponding Majorana modes. If one is solely concerned with the fermionic spectrum -- that is, the values of $\varepsilon_j$ -- one can simply diagonalise the Hermitian matrix $iA$. This matrix has particle hole symmetry, so the eigenvalues come in pairs $\pm \varepsilon_j$ where $\varepsilon_j$ correspond exactly to the energies of the fermionic modes given above.

In a system with $n_p$ plaquettes, we have $2^{n_p}$ possible flux sectors ($2^{n_p-1}$ in periodic boundaries), and in general it is a non-trivial task to determine which flux sector corresponds to the lowest ground state energy. However, on the honeycomb lattice the ground state was shown by Lieb to be found by setting $\phi_p=+1$ for all plaquettes~\cite{lieb_flux_1994}. Thus, the ground state can always be found by bipartitioning the sites into an $A$ and $B$ sublattice, and setting the bond operators such that $u_{jk}$ always points from each $A$ site towards its three neighbouring $B$ sites (this was the `background' flux sector depicted in Fig.~\ref{fig:flux_flips}). In the following, we shall refer to this flux configuration as the {\it canonical flux sector.}

Thus, we see that our interacting spin system has been divided into two separate degrees of freedom: a `classical' $\mathbb Z_2$ flux degree of freedom corresponding to the bond operators and plaquette fluxes, and a fermionic degree determined by the Majorana spectrum in a given flux sector. In what follows we will distinguish between these two aspects as the `flux' and `fermionic' properties of the system. For example, the flux gap is defined as the difference in energy between the ground state in the canonical sector and the ground state energy in any other flux sector. The fermionic gap, however, is determined by the lowest fermionic energy $\varepsilon_j$ in the canonical flux sector.

\subsection{The Ground-State Phase Diagram}

We finish this review with a brief discussion of the ground-state phase diagram, which we shall explore by varying the three $J^\alpha$ parameters. Changing the sign of each individual $J^\alpha$ has no effect on the system, since a change in sign can always be countered by flipping the value of all $u$ values on the corresponding bonds. A completely general two-dimensional phase diagram can be constructed by considering only values of $J^\alpha$ on the triangle defined by $\sum_\alpha J^\alpha = 1$, $J^\alpha \geq 0$.

        \textbf{(a)}~Close to the corners of the triangle, one set of bonds is much stronger than the other two. Thus, the system effectively dimerises on the strong bonds, with the remaining two couplings acting on the dimerised sites as a weak perturbation. 
        \textbf{(b)}~In the centre of one of the sides of the triangle, two sets of bonds are much stronger than the third. In this case, the system decomposes into a set of disconnected chain models, which individually have gapless dispersion. The remaining coupling serves to weakly couple adjacent chains.
        \textbf{(c)}~At the centre of the triangle all bonds are equally strong, and the system is in some sense maximally frustrated. Around this point we have a gapless phase. If a magnetic field is used to open a gap, the system forms a quantum spin liquid phase, which supports anyon excitation with non-Abelian braiding statistics.

In the canonical flux sector, the Hamiltonian (Eqn.~\ref{eqn:majorana_hamiltonian_with_u}) has translational symmetry. Thus, the fermionic spectrum can be calculated by transforming to momentum space, obtaining a Bloch Hamiltonian of the form
\begin{align}\label{eqn:kitaev_bloch_hamiltonian}
    iA(\textbf q) = \textbf d(\textbf q) \cdot \bm \sigma,
\end{align}
defined by the Pauli vector,
\begin{align} \label{eqn:no_mag_field_d}
    \textbf{d}(\textbf q) = -\begin{pmatrix}
        J^x \sin q_x +J^y \sin q_y \\
        J^x \cos q_x + J^y \cos q_y + J^z \\ 0
    \end{pmatrix}
\end{align}
The phase diagram of this Hamiltonian has two distinct sectors determined by whether the Pauli vector defined above sweeps over the origin or not, shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:honey_phase_triangle}. Around each corner of the triangle, in the regime where one of the $J$ parameters is larger than the sum of the other two, $iA(\textbf q)$ defines a gapped system, which we label as the $A_\alpha$ phase. When the $J$-parameters satisfy $J^\alpha \leq J^\beta + J^\gamma$ for all permutations of indices, the dispersion has two Dirac cones, and the system is in a gapless phase, which we label the $B$ phase. 

\subsubsection{The Gapped \textit{A} Phases}

The three gapped phases are best understood by considering the model very close to the corners of the triangle. Here, one of the couplings is orders of magnitude stronger than the other two. For example, in Fig.~\ref{fig:honey_phase_triangle}a, we depict the limit $J^z \gg J^y,J^x$. Let us consider without loss of generality this limit. Here, the Hamiltonian may be treated using perturbation theory, where the $x$ and $y$-bonds are treated as a perturbation of a ground state calculated only from the $z$-part of the Hamiltonian. 

Since the $\sigma^z\sigma^z$ couplings dominate, we expect the two spins on each bond to arrange into a $\ket{\uparrow \uparrow}$ or $\ket{\downarrow \downarrow}$ configuration, thus effectively combining, forming a single spin on each $z$-bond. Each of these effective spins is then coupled to four neighbours with $ \sigma^x\sigma^x$ and $\sigma^y\sigma^y$ interactions -- forming a square lattice. Here we will omit a full discussion of the perturbation theory, however it was shown that this model can be exactly mapped onto the toric code Hamiltonian at lowest order in perturbation theory~\cite{kitaev_anyons_2006,kitaev_fault-tolerant_2003,schmidt_emergent_2008,dusuel_creation_2008,vidal_perturbative_2008}. Thus, this phase inherits the properties of the toric code, including the presence of Abelian anyonic excitation.

Around the centre of the phase diagram all $J^\alpha$ are comparably strong, so the system can be considered maximally frustrated, and thus the best candidate for a strongly entangled ground state. In this phase the fermionic Hamiltonian is gapless, and so excitations have long-ranged interactions, preventing them from having well-defined exchange statistics. Thus, let us introduce a small perturbation capable of gapping out the Dirac cones in the dispersion relation of Eqn.~\ref{eqn:kitaev_bloch_hamiltonian}. 

Any perturbation that respects time reversal symmetry is unsuitable for this task. This is because, as we discussed in \textsection\ref{sec:hex_trs}, a time-reversal symmetric perturbation can only introduce couplings between sites on different sublattices. For a Bloch Hamiltonian of the form $iA(\textbf q) = f_x (\textbf q) \sigma^x + f_y (\textbf q) \sigma^y$, such a perturbation will only serve to modify the form of $f_x$ and $f_y$, shifting the position of the Dirac cones in phase space. However, the Pauli vector will remain confined to the $x$-$y$ plane, so a small perturbation will not be sufficient to prevent it from crossing the origin as it sweeps out the full Brillouin zone. Rather, we must introduce a perturbation that breaks TRS, and couples pairs of $A$ or $B$ sites. This introduces a $\sigma^z$-type term to the Bloch Hamiltonian. One example of such a perturbation corresponds to applying a weak magnetic field to our system. A straightforward magnetic field term of the form $V = h_\alpha \sigma^\alpha$ destroys the integrability of the system, however using perturbation theory one can construct an effective magnetic field term that respects it. The lowest term in perturbation theory that breaks TRS is found at third order, given by a term of the form
\begin{align}
    V_{\textup{eff}} = - \kappa\, 
    \sigma_j^x
    \sigma_k^y
    \sigma_l^z,
\end{align}
with $\kappa = \frac{h_xh_yh_z}{J^2} $, acting over adjacent triples of sites. We shall omit the details of this calculation since it shall not be relevant to the amorphous case. Rather we just state the result, which is that the perturbation has the effect of introducing a complex next-nearest neighbour coupling to the Majorana Hamiltonian, such that the Pauli vector is modified to
 \begin{align}\label{eqn:d_shifted}
    \textbf{d}(\textbf q) = -\begin{pmatrix}
        J^x \sin q_x +J^y \sin q_y \\
        J^x \cos q_x + J^y \cos q_y + J^z \\ 
        4\kappa[\sin q_x - \sin q_y + \sin(q_x - q_y)].
    \end{pmatrix}
\end{align}
As can be seen in Fig.~\ref{fig:honeycomb_gaps}, this perturbation is enough to gap the two Dirac cones.



\subsection{The Chern Number} \label{sec:chern_number_and_anyon_stats}

We finish the review of the Honeycomb model by discussing the topological properties of the model in the A and B phases. This has two parts. First we will consider the Chern number of the Majorana Hamiltonian in each case, before discussing the effect of the Chern number on the fractionalised excitations of the model in the $B$ phase. 

In this model, the Chern number is calculated as a property of the fermionic Hamiltonian, defined by the matrix $iA$. Of course, we could explicitly calculate the Chern number using the machinery presented in \textsection\ref{sec:Abelian Berry Phase and the Chern Number}. However, we can instead infer the Chern number directly from the form of the $\textbf d(\textbf q)$ vector that defines the Bloch Hamiltonian, given by Eqn.~\ref{eqn:no_mag_field_d}. This is because -- as discussed in \textsection\ref{sec:an_example} -- in a two-level system we can find the Chern number by just counting the number of times the surface swept out by $\textbf d$ encloses the origin. In the $A$ phases, this surface is confined to the $x$-$y$ plane. Furthermore, since the system is gapped, $\textbf d$ does not ever cross the origin. This means that the surface cannot enclose the origin and thus the $A$ phase has $\mathcal Q = 0$. In the $B$ phase, $\textbf d(\textbf q)$ is also confined to the $x$-$y$ plane, and crosses the origin at two points in phase space, which we label with $ \textbf q_*$ and $-\textbf q_*$, since they are related by reflection over the origin. Once the magnetic perturbation is added, we find that $\textbf d$ is shifted away from the origin in the $z$-direction at these two points. Furthermore, the $z$-position, given in Eqn.~\ref{eqn:d_shifted} is an odd function of $\textbf q$. This ensures that one of the Dirac cones is gapped with $\textbf d$ avoiding the origin with positive $d_z$, whereas the other Dirac cone is gapped with $\textbf d$ avoiding the origin with negative $d_z$. Thus, the surface swept out by $\textbf d$ encloses the origin once, and the Chern number in this phase is given by $\mathcal Q = \pm 1$, where the sign is determined by the sign of $\kappa$.

When the Chern number is odd, i.e.~when it is $\pm 1$, the presence of a flipped plaquette binds a Majorana zero mode around that plaquette. A rigorous proof of this statement is provided by Kitaev in Appendix C of \cite{kitaev_anyons_2006}, which we shall not restate. Rather, let us provide an intuitive argument for why this must be the case. The first step is to turn our Majorana Hamiltonian into an equivalent fermionic system. This is done by overlaying two copies of the system, labelled with $c'_j$ and $c''_j$ respectively, without coupling them to one another. Thus, the Hamiltonian (Eqn.~\ref{eqn:majorana_hamiltonian_with_u}) becomes
\begin{align}
    H = \frac i 4 \sum_{jk} A_{jk} (c'_jc'_k + c''_jc''_k).
\end{align}
By constructing a fermion operator as $a_j = \frac 12 (c'_j + i c''_j)$, we can then rewrite this as 
\begin{align}
    H = \sum_{jk} iA_{jk} a^\dag_j a_k.
\end{align}
Thus, the system has become a straightforward fermionic hopping hamiltonian. Note that because the hopping matrix $iA_{jk}$ is pure imaginary, the Hamiltonian anti-commutes with the complex conjugation operator, so has particle-hole symmetry. Since the hopping Hamiltonian is changed only by a factor of four, the Chern number we calculated for the Majorana case is the same for the doubled fermion system -- this Hamiltonian is just a standard Chern insulator!

Now, let us consider the spectrum of the system with a single flipped plaquette in the centre, as shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:bound_states}a, which has been created by flipping a horizontal line of spins that starts at the plaquette and extends to infinity. To see how a bound state arises, let us consider creating this system by sandwiching together two Chern insulators. We divide the system such that the line of flipped bond operators lies along the seam between the two halves, meaning that each Chern insulator is individually clean and uniform. Thus, the flipped plaquette is found in between the two halved systems. Now, in order to couple the two systems together, let us introduce hoppings between the two systems such that the coupling parameter changes sign as it crosses the flipped plaquette, as shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:bound_states}b. 

Before the coupling is introduced, each Chern insulator must have a single edge mode propagating along the boundary. Furthermore, as we showed in \textsection\ref{sec:QWZ_strip_geometry}, we can expect that this edge mode will have linear dispersion as it crosses the Fermi energy. Thus, in the long-wavelength limit, we may approximate the Hamiltonian for the edge mode as $H \approx -iv\partial_x$, where $v$ is a parameter that determines the group velocity of the edge mode. On the upper Chern insulator, the velocity will be positive, and on the lower insulator the velocity will be negative. Thus, the effective Hamiltonian describing the two uncoupled edge modes is given by
\begin{align}
    H = -iv\sigma^z\partial_x.
\end{align}
Now let us introduce the coupling between the two half-systems. Since we must respect the overall particle-hole symmetry of the system, the only allowed coupling must be proportional to $\sigma^y$ -- the Hamiltonian must remain pure imaginary and antisymmetric. Thus, the overall Hamiltonian is
\begin{align}
    H = -iv\sigma^z\partial_x + \mu(x) \sigma^y,
\end{align}
where $\mu(x)$ is a parameter that changes sign exactly at the point where the flux is placed, let us assume that $\mu$ is $+1$ on the left and $-1$ on the right. The existence of a zero mode can be determined by finding a solution to $H \ket \psi = 0$, which is straightforwardly given by a state of the form $\ket \psi = \mathcal N e^{\pm \frac x v} \ket - $, where the sign in the exponential changes depending on whether $x$ is to the left or right of the flux. Thus, we have an exponentially localised zero mode appearing. In general, such a mode will always appear anywhere that the sign of $\mu$ changes. 

As we have seen, the fermionic Chern insulator binds a single fermionic zero mode, exponentially localised around the plaquette with a flux threaded through. All that remains is to remember that the fermionic system was really composed of two uncoupled Majorana systems. This means that the fermionic operator representing the zero mode can now be split back into two Majorana modes, $a_0 = \frac 12 (c'_0 + ic''_0)$. Each of these Majoranas must reside on one of the two overlaid systems, so we see that in the original Kitaev model, a $\mathbb Z_2$ flux excitation will always bind a Majorana zero mode, provided that the system has an odd Chern number. In Fig.~\ref{fig:bound_states}c, we show an example of two fluxes, with the local density of the Majorana mode around each flux, and in Fig.~\ref{fig:bound_states}d we show how the energy of the flux-bound Majoranas decreases exponentially as the distance between fluxes is increased.
