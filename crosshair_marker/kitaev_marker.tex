\section{Kitaev's Local Marker}
\label{sec:kitaev_marker}
The majority of accounts of local markers tend to focus on the development of the Bott index and the Chern marker. However, the first construction of a real-space Chern number can (along with the rest of modern physics) be found in the appendices of a seminal work by Kitaev~\cite{kitaev_anyons_2006}. Thus, we shall start our discussion with an explanation of Kitaev's local marker, the discussion will largely follow the source material, however we provide some additional detail at various points.

    \end{center}
     \captionof{figure}[Partition of the plane into three regions used to construct Kitaev's local Chern number.]{
     Diagram showing the partition of the plane into three regions labelled with $A$, $B$ and $C$. An example of $h_{jkl}$ is depicted with red dots, where arrows indicate the positive direction. 
     }\label{fig:h_jkl}
\end{figure}%
 
As always, our starting point is a Hamiltonian defined on an arbitrary lattice with sites at positions $\textbf r_j$, however in this case we shall work with an infinite system. Here, let us define the projector onto a band $P = \sum_{\textup{band}} \ketbra{\psi}{\psi}$, along with a shorthand for off-diagonal correlations given by 
\begin{align}
    P_{jk} = \braket{\textbf r_j |P| \textbf r_k}.
\end{align}
In general, if this is onto a gapped set of states, then the projector will satisfy the following condition~\cite{hastings_spectral_2006},
\begin{align}
    |P_{jk}| \leq c|\textbf r_j - \textbf r_k|^{-\alpha},
\end{align}
for any $\alpha$ greater than the dimensionality of the system. Following Kitaev, let us define a 2-current,
\begin{align}
    h_{jkl} = 12\pi i (P_{jk}P_{kl}P_{lj} - P_{jl}P_{lk}P_{kj}).
\end{align}
We divide our system into three regions in real space, $A$, $B$ and $C$, and define the real-space Chern number,
\begin{align}
    \qkit(P) = \sum_{j \in A}\sum_{k \in B}\sum_{l \in C} h_{jkl},
\end{align}
which we can re-express in terms of a set of projectors onto the three regions,
\begin{align} \label{eqn:qkit_def}
    \qkit(P) = 12\pi i \tr( PAPBPC - PCPBPA) .
\end{align}
This quantity remains invariant when we relabel any finite area from one region to another. Additionally, due to the localised nature of $P$, the only nonzero contributions to it come from around the point where the three regions $A$, $B$ and $C$ meet. 

\begin{shaded}
    {\it The Invariance of $\qkit$ to changes in $A$, $B$ and $C$ --- } In order to see why this is the case, let us consider picking up some finite chunk of the region $B$, which we label with $\alpha$ and moving it over into region $A$. Thus, let us make the redefinition
    \begin{align}
        A' &= A + \alpha \\
        B' & = B - \alpha
    \end{align}
    \begin{center}
        \includegraphics[]{crosshair_marker/figures/alpha_region.pdf}
    \end{center}
    Thus, the matrix term in the Kitaev Chern number becomes
    \begin{align}
        PAPBPC - h.c. = PA'PB'PC 
        + PAP\alpha PC 
        - P\alpha PB'PC - h.c.
    \end{align}
    Now let us use the identity $B' = \1 - \alpha - A - C$ to re-express this in the form
    \begin{align}\begin{split}
        PAPBPC - h.c. = & PA'PB'PC 
        + PAP \alpha P C
        + P\alpha PAPC \\ 
        & - P\alpha PC
        + P\alpha P\alpha PC
        + P\alpha PCPC  - h.c.
    \end{split}
    \end{align}
Here we have used the identity $P^2 = P$ to obtain this line. All the extra terms on the RHS vanish when the trace is taken. Note that generally one cannot use the cyclic property of the trace here, since our operators are infinite matrices, so there is no guarantee that the rearranged infinite sum converges to the same value. However, since $\alpha$ represents a finite region, the sum vanishes far from $\alpha$, and so we are free to cycle any trace containing $\alpha$. Thus, we are left with
\begin{align}\begin{split}
    \tr (PAPBPC - h.c.) = \tr (PA'PB'PC  - h.c.).
\end{split}
\end{align}
In fact, the extra finite region $\alpha$ need not be a projector, since the above proof holds regardless of the properties of $\alpha$ provided that it has finite support. Thus, we can go even further and see that the value of $\qkit$ is invariant under the addition of any finite matrix to one of out three regions, provided we subtract it from another region as well.


\end{shaded}  

Let us re-express $\qkit$ to a form that will be useful in the following discussion. Since the expression is invariant under cycling of the order of our three regions, we may write it as
\begin{align}
    \qkit = 4 \pi i \tr(
        &PAPBPC + PBPCPA + PCPAPB \\
        &- PAPCPB - PCPBPA - PBPAPC),
\end{align}
note that we are not allowed to cycle the terms in the trace here. Inserting the identity 
\begin{align}\label{eqn:cab_identity}
    C = \1 - A - B,
\end{align} 
we arrive at the following expression,
\begin{align} \label{eqn:qkit_as_commutator}
    \qkit(P) = 4 \pi i \tr[
        PAP, PBP
    ]
\end{align}
This is a trace of a commutator, which will always vanish if one of the operators in the commutator has finite support. This supports our assertion that $\qkit$ is invariant under addition of any finite matrix to $A \rightarrow A + \alpha$, since $\tr[P\alpha P, PBP]$ vanishes. Of course, we must also transform either $B \rightarrow B-\alpha$ or $C \rightarrow C-\alpha$ for the identity \ref{eqn:cab_identity} to hold.

This invariance to the form of $A, B$ and $C$ affords us with an astounding amount of freedom in how we choose to define this Chern number. Not only can we select any three regions with any shape (provided the overall topology of the three regions far from the triple contact point remains the same), but we can even blur the boundaries between the projectors or allow them to overlap and nothing will change.

\subsection{Relation to the Chern Number}

So far we have constructed a quantity, $\qkit$ and shown that there is a remarkable amount of freedom in how we can modify it without changing its value. In order to connect this to band topology, we will need to show that when evaluated in a translationally symmetric context, $\qkit$ corresponds exactly to the Chern number. Let us start by re-expressing $\qkit$ again in a new form. We define a set of step function projectors $\vartheta_x, \vartheta_y$ that satisfy 
\begin{align}
    \vartheta_\alpha \ket{\textbf r} = 
    \begin{cases}
        \ket {\textbf r}&\textup{ for }r_{\alpha} \geq 0,\\ 
        0&\textup{ for }r_{\alpha} < 0.
    \end{cases}
\end{align} 
Let us now consider the operator
\begin{align}
    2 \pi i \tr
    [ P\vartheta_x P, P\vartheta_y P ].
\end{align}
By decomposing the step function into projectors onto the four quarters of the plane, we arrive at
\begin{align}\label{eqn:qkit_abcd}
    \begin{split}
    2\pi i\tr [P \vartheta_xP, P \vartheta_yP ] = 2\pi i \tr \big ( 
        &[P a P, P b P ] + 
        [P b P, P b P ] \\
        &+[P a P, P c P ] 
        +[P b P, P c P ]
        \big )    
\end{split}
\end{align}
\begin{figure}
    \begin{center}
       \includegraphics[]{crosshair_marker/figures/four_boxes.pdf}
    \end{center}
    \captionof{figure}[Decomposition of the plane into four quadrants.]{
    The decomposition of the two-step functions proposed in Eqn.~\ref{eqn:qkit_abcd} into a set of four projectors on the quarters of the plane. The $x$-step function is composed of regions $b$ and $c$, and the $y$-step function is composed of regions $a$ and $b$.
    }\label{fig:four_squares}
\end{figure}%
Clearly the second term on the RHS vanishes. The third term also vanishes, which can be seen by its invariance under a mirror symmetry across the line $y +x = 0$ (a mirror reflection will always flip the sign of $h_{jkl}$). Finally, we see that the first and last terms are both equal to $\qkit/2$, thus
\begin{align}\label{eqn:qkit_step_functions}
    \qkit = 2\pi i\tr [P \vartheta_xP, P \vartheta_yP ].
\end{align}
In order to make contact with momentum space, we shall work in an infinite translationally invariant system. Clearly, in this context the position of the step functions will have no effect on the value of $\qkit$. Thus, let us define a set of translated step functions
\begin{align}
    \vartheta_{R_\alpha} \ket{\textbf r} = 
    \begin{cases}
        \ket {\textbf r}&\textup{ for }r_{\alpha} \geq R_\alpha,\\ 
        0&\textup{ for }r_{\alpha} < R_\alpha.
    \end{cases}
\end{align} 
Obviously, the value of $\qkit(\textbf R) = 2\pi i\tr [P \vartheta_{R_x} P, P \vartheta_{R_y}P ]$ is the same as $\qkit$. Thus, let us choose some (arbitrarily large) square region of area $L^2$ the plane and take the average of $\qkit(\textbf R)$ over this region, 
\begin{align}
    \qkit &= \frac{1}{L^2} \int_{-L/2}^{L/2} dR_x\, dR_y\, \qkit(\textbf R)  \\ 
    & = \frac{2\pi i}{L^2} \tr [ PxP, PyP ] \\ 
    & = \frac{2\pi i}{L^2} \tr P \big[ [x,P],[y,P] \big ] 
\end{align}
In the limit of large $L$, we expect that the contribution to the trace is uniform in space, and so can replace the global trace with a local trace around some arbitrary point in the system $\textbf r$, $\frac1 {L^2} \tr \rightarrow \loctr_{\textbf{r}} $. Thus, we arrive at 
\begin{align}\label{eqn:chern_marker_from_kitaev}
    \qkit = 2\pi i \loctr _{\textbf{r}} \big [P[x,P],[y,P]\big ]. 
\end{align}
Now let us construct the exact form of the projector onto a band for a translationally symmetric Hamiltonian. From Bloch's theorem, we know the projector will have the following form
\begin{align}
    P = \frac{1}{4\pi^2}\int d^2 \textbf k \ketbra k k \otimes \psi(\textbf k)
\end{align}
This means that we can use the identity $[x, P] \rightarrow -i \partial_x \psi(\textbf k)$, where $\partial_x$ represents a derivative with respect to $k_x$, to show that 
\begin{align}
    \qkit = \frac{-i}{2\pi} \int d^2 \textbf k \, 
    \loctr _{\textbf{r}} \big (\ketbra {\textbf k} {\textbf k} \otimes
        \psi(\textbf k) [\partial_x \psi(\textbf k), \partial_y \psi(\textbf k)]
    \big )
\end{align}
Now, using the fact that $ \braket{\textbf r \ketbra{\textbf k}{\textbf k}  \textbf r}  = 1$, this reduces to a trace over the unit cell degree of freedom only,
\begin{align}
    \qkit = \frac{-i}{2\pi} \int d^2 \textbf k \, 
    \tr \big (
        \psi(\textbf k) [\partial_x \psi(\textbf k), \partial_y \psi(\textbf k)]
    \big ).
\end{align} 
Comparing to Eqn.~\ref{eqn:operator_berry_curvature}, we see that this quantity exactly evaluates an integral of the Berry curvature over the Brillouin zone. Thus, we have shown that -- at least in the context of an infinite, translationally symmetric system -- $\qkit$ evaluates the Chern number.

\begin{shaded}
    {\it A Useful Identity for Operators in Momentum Space --- } Let us start by considering an arbitrary operator in a translationally symmetric system with the following form,
    \begin{align}
        A =  \int_{BZ} \frac{d k}{2\pi} \ketbra k k \otimes A(k),
    \end{align}
    where $A(k)$ acts on the degrees of freedom inside the unit cell. Here we use a 1D Brillouin zone, but this can be straightforwardly extended to 
    higher dimensions. Let us consider the following expression,
    \begin{align}
        [x, A] = \frac{1}{2\pi} \int_{BZ} d k \big [x,\ketbra k k \big ] \otimes A(k).
    \end{align} 
    Using the identity $x\ket k = i\partial_k \ket k$, we can rewrite this in the form
    \begin{align}
        [x, A] & = \frac{1}{2\pi} \int_{BZ} d k\, i \partial_k \big(\ketbra k k \big) \otimes A(k) \\ 
        &= \frac{1}{2\pi} \int_{BZ} d k\,  \ketbra k k 
        \otimes ( -i \partial_k A(k)),
    \end{align} 
    where we used the fact that an integral of total derivative of the RHS vanishes, as the integral is over the whole Brillouin zone. Thus, we can make the general substitution
    \begin{align}
        [x,A] \rightarrow -i \partial_{k} A(k)
    \end{align}
\end{shaded}

\subsection{Kitaev's Marker in a Finite System} \label{sec:finite_kitaev}

\begin{figure}
     \begin{center}
        \includegraphics[]{crosshair_marker/figures/finite_kitaev_issues.pdf}
    \end{center}
     \captionof{figure}[Two examples of the vanishing of Kitaev's local marker in a finite system.]{
      Two examples depicting how $\qkit$ vanishes in finite systems. \textbf{(Left)} A topological system in open boundary conditions with necessarily have gapless edge modes present (depicted here with a blue border), which contribute a term to $\qkit$ that exactly cancels out the topological value around the triple contact point. \textbf{(Right)} An example partition of the torus (shown with opposite edges identified) into three regions. In this case, four triple contact points are present. Around each two have the regions arranged clockwise around them and two counter-clockwise, so overall $\qkit$ vanishes.
     }\label{fig:finite_kitaev_issues}
    \end{figure}%
So far, we have defined Kitaev's Chern number for a system with infinite boundary conditions. This version of the Chern number is useful in analytical arguments -- for example, a similar quantity was used to prove the quantisation of Hall conductivity in the presence of disorder~\cite{bellissard_noncommutative_1994}. However, to be of broad practical use, $\qkit$ must still work in a finite system with open or periodic boundary conditions. Our current definition is unsuitable, which can be seen from Eqn.~\ref{eqn:qkit_as_commutator}. Since the trace of a commutator vanishes in a finite system, this quantity is only nonzero in infinite boundary conditions. 

    Before we modify $\qkit$, let us first consider the details of how it misbehaves in finite systems. The stability of $\qkit$ depends on two assumptions -- that the projector $P$ is exponentially localised, and that the three regions $A$, $B$, $C$ all touch at a single point only. In a finite system, at least one of these two assumptions will always fail. In the case of the localisation of the projector, as long as the band remains gapped, we can expect that this condition will hold. However, in a system with nonzero Chern number and open boundaries, the bulk boundary correspondence forces the existence of gapless edge modes. This means that the topological bulk is surrounded by a gapless region where the projector no longer has short-ranged correlations, allowing for a contribution to Eqn.~\ref{eqn:qkit_def} that cancels out the central contribution from the triple contact point. In periodic boundaries we have no edge modes, however it is impossible to partition the torus into three regions with only a single point where the three meet. Instead, triple contact points are always created in pairs. Furthermore, of these pairs, one contact point always has the regions ordered clockwise and the other counter-clockwise around the point. This means that the contributions to $\qkit$ from the two points will always cancel out perfectly. An example of each of these effects is shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:finite_kitaev_issues}. 
    \begin{figure}
        \centering
         \begin{center}
            \includegraphics[]{crosshair_marker/figures/restricted_regions.pdf}
        \end{center}
         \captionof{figure}[The restricted Kitaev marker, suitable for use in finite systems.]{
        Three restricted regions for which $\qkit$ can be calculated. Now there is only one point where the three regions meet. Provided that $P$ remains short ranged in the circular region and the distance from the triple contact point to the boundary of the circle is larger than the correlation length of $P$, this will calculate a quantised Chern number.
         }\label{fig:restricted_kitaev}
    \end{figure}%

In order to remedy this, let us restrict the three regions over which the Chern number is calculated, such that the boundaries and other triple contact points are avoided, as shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:restricted_kitaev}. In this case, there is only a single point where $A$, $B$ and $C$ meet. Let us define a projector onto the circular region as $O$. In this case, the finite version of $\qkit$ is defined the same as Eqn.~\ref{eqn:qkit_def}, however with the following redefinition of the projectors
\begin{align}
    A \rightarrow \tilde A = OA.
\end{align}
The restricted Chern number can be written as
\begin{align} \label{eqn:qkit_def_restricted}
    \qkit^O = 12\pi i \tr( 
        P\tilde AP\tilde BP\tilde C - 
        P\tilde CP\tilde BP\tilde A).
\end{align}
Using the identity
\begin{align}
    \tilde C = O - \tilde A - \tilde B,
\end{align}
we can re-express this similarly to Eqn.~\ref{eqn:qkit_as_commutator},
\begin{align}
    \qkit^O = 4 \pi i \tr_O[
        P\tilde AP, P\tilde BP
    ],
\end{align}
where the trace is taken only over the region $O$. Finally, assuming that the boundary of O is sufficiently far from the triple contact point (or any region where $P$ is long-ranged), we can expect that there is no contribution near the boundary of $O$, we can drop the tilde on $A$ and $B$ to get,
\begin{align} \label{eqn:qkit_finite_step}
    \qkit^O = 4 \pi i \tr_O[
        P AP, P BP
    ].
\end{align}
The value of the marker is not dependent to the exact shape of $O$, provided its boundary avoids any other triple contact points, or gap closings.

\subsection{Numerical Examples}

Let us now provide a slide show of numerical examples of Kitaev's Chern number calculated in the amorphous QWZ model, verifying the arguments presented above. In each case, we calculate the step-function form of the Chern number given in Eqn.~\ref{eqn:qkit_step_functions}. Rather than immediately summing over a bulk region, we shall consider the real-space contribution to the trace by finding the local trace of the marker, $\tr_{\textbf r} ( ... ) = \tr \braket{\textbf r | ... | \textbf r}$. Thus, we evaluate the local contribution given by
\begin{align}
    \qkit (\textbf r) = 4 \pi i \tr_{\textbf r}[
        P \vartheta_x P, P \vartheta_y P
    ].
\end{align}
By plotting $\qkit (\textbf r)$ over the system, it is easy to see where the central contribution to $\qkit$ ends, and thus deduce what area to trace over to find a quantised Chern number. 

In each case, we also calculate the trace of the marker over a circular region, $O$, around the central peak, varying the radius of the circular region. We expect that as the region is increased, the trace should quickly converge to a near-quantised number. Once the circle becomes large enough to approach the edges of the system, the sum approaches zero as the trace becomes a trace over the whole system. 

We show three sets of examples. In Fig.~\ref{fig:qkit_example_boundaries} we compare the marker value in periodic boundary conditions and open boundary conditions, giving a direct example of the picture presented in \textsection\ref{sec:finite_kitaev}, where we discuss the two ways that finite size effects can cause the full trace to vanish. In Fig.~\ref{fig:qkit_phase_transition} we plot the marker while tuning the QWZ model from a topological phase to a trivial phase. This is done by tuning the on-site splitting parameter $u$ (see Eqn.~\ref{eqn:amorphous_qwz_hamiltonian}) over the phase transition at $u = -1.5$. Finally, in Fig.~\ref{fig:qkit_disorder}, we add disorder by locally varying the value of $u$ with a perturbation drawn from a normal distribution. By increasing the standard deviation, we see how the marker changes as disorder gradually destroys the topological phase.
