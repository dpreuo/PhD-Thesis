
In 1928, just two years after Erwin Schr\"odinger published his famous wave equation, Felix Bloch proposed a new method for calculating the electronic properties of solids \cite{bloch_uber_1929}. By this point, the idea that electrons in atoms arranged themselves into a hierarchy of discrete orbitals was well-established. Bloch proposed that, rather than starting with a free electron and considering how its motion might be affected by the a material's potential, the properties might be better understood by starting with a set of electronic states $\ket{\phi_n(\textbf{R}_i)}$, corresponding to the $n^{\textup{th}}$ orbitals on the atom at position $\textbf{R}_i$. Here, a wavefunction could be constructed by taking a linear combination of these orbitals. Next, the Hamiltonian operator, given by 
\begin{align} \label{eqn:schrodinger_H}
    H = \frac{\textbf{p}^2}{2m} + V
\end{align}
would be rewritten in terms of these states. By exploiting the translational symmetry in crystalline materials and considering only the lowest energy $s$ orbital, Bloch was able to solve the periodic Schr\"odinger equation and the technique -- known as the LCAO (linear combination of atomic orbitals), or tight-binding method -- paved the way for the development of band theory.

In practice, this was a spectacularly complex procedure to perform rigorously. To re-express the Hamiltonian in terms of orbitals, one had to evaluate an enormous number of integrals of the form $\braket{\phi_n(\textbf{R}_i)|H|\phi_m(\textbf{R}_j)}$ -- one for each pair of orbitals separated by each lattice translation. Thus, finding the exact form of the Hamiltonian was considered an impossible task. Rather, the utility of this method became apparent when one accepted this impossibility and just guessed the form of $H$ \cite{slater_simplified_1954,harrison_tight-binding_1994}\footnote{In \cite{slater_simplified_1954}, Slater and Koster provide a detailed account of the many complications involved with performing a rigorous LCAO calculation. For the gossip inclined, the paper also has a couple of spectacularly combative paragraphs dedicated to naming various physicists in the field just to explain the ways that their proposals are `of no value'.}. 
With just a few assumptions one could simplify the Hamiltonian to the point that it depended on only a small number of parameters. For example, one would expect the coupling between two orbitals to vanish unless they were sufficiently close as to overlap. Furthermore, by studying the symmetries in a crystalline material, one finds many couplings that must be equivalent. Thus, the game of constructing a tight binding model for a particular material became one of adjusting this small number of parameters until the model matched the properties found in experiment.

Although we started with the original form of Schr\"odinger's Hamiltonian (eqn.~\ref{eqn:schrodinger_H}), the tight-binding picture we have arrived at is a radically different world for an electron to live in. In Schr\"odinger's story, the electron exists as a wave in a fundamentally smooth world, where the potential defines a landscape through which the electron roams. However, in Bloch's story the collective properties of the electrons emerge from the overlap between a discrete lattice of orbitals. Here the landscape is more akin to an archipelago of islands, with electrons that can hop from island to island as in fig.~\ref{fig:hopping_electron}. Constructing a model in this formalism is a completely different procedure to that of Schr\"odinger, since we must start by asking: \textit{What is the geometry of the lattice?} Only then can we decide the values of the couplings between lattice sites and build a Hamiltonian. Here, the geometry of the lattice has a fundamental role in deciding what kind of Hamiltonians we can build.

Of course, the tight binding method is just one of a huge variety of approximations that have been used to understand how electrons behave in materials. However, it is not the only context where the lattice geometry turns out to be absolutely central in deciding what kinds of Hamiltonians can exist, and what kind of collective phases may emerge. For example, in a crystalline material, the lattice structure determines the shape of the Brillouin zone, which inherits its symmetries. Thus the band structure that emerges is strongly constrained by the shape of the lattice \cite{simon_oxford_2013,nussbaum_crystal_1966}. In a Mott insulator the role of lattice geometry becomes even more important. Here the electron-electron repulsion is sufficiently strong that electrons become immobilised on each site. An effective description of the material treats each particle as a point on a lattice, and electrons interact only by realigning their spin and internal degrees of freedom in response to the state of a neighbour \cite{mott_basis_1949,roy_mott_2019}.

In condensed matter, the process of learning a new model can often feel similar to learning a board game: One starts by looking at the board, studying its symmetries and defects, and then progresses to learning the rules and finally playing the game. A natural question to ask is: \textit{How does the game depend on the shape of the board?} The equivalent question for a physicist is: \textit{How does the microscopic structure of a material determine the types of collective phases that can emerge?} This term, `microscopics', can refer to a variety of properties, we can change the lattice structure, adding in holes, defects or altering its symmetries and its connectivity, or we can introduce a disordered potential, adding an energy penalty to occupying certain regions over others. Over the last hundred years, this question has yielded an enormous variety of answers. In some contexts, for example the study of phase transitions or topological insulators, the answer has often been that the microscopics do not matter. Here the same model constructed in a variety of contexts will lead to largely identical physics. On the other hand there are cases -- notably that of frustrated magnetism -- where the details of the lattice geometry are fundamental in determining the properties of the system. In this thesis, our line of inquiry will be guided by this question. We shall study topological phases of matter, specifically Chern insulators and Kitaev spin liquids and ask which aspects of these phases are sensitive to these microscopic details, and which are able to transcend them.
