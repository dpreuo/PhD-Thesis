
In 1928, just two years after Erwin Schr\"odinger published his famous wave equation, Felix Bloch proposed a new method for calculating the electronic properties of solids \cite{bloch_uber_1929}. By this point, the idea that electrons in atoms arranged themselves into a hierarchy of discrete orbitals was well-established. Bloch proposed that, rather than starting with a free electron and considering how its motion might be affected by the material's potential, the properties might be better understood by starting with a set of electronic states $\ket{\phi_n(\textbf{R}_i)}$, each corresponding to the $n^{\textup{th}}$ orbital on the atom at position $\textbf{R}_i$. Here, a wavefunction could be constructed by taking a linear combination of these orbitals. Next, the Hamiltonian operator, given by 
\begin{align} \label{eqn:schrodinger_H}
    H = \frac{\textbf{p}^2}{2m} + V
\end{align}
would be rewritten in terms of these states. By exploiting the translational symmetry in crystalline materials and considering only the lowest energy $s$ orbital, Bloch was able to solve the periodic Schr\"odinger equation and the technique -- known as the LCAO (linear combination of atomic orbitals), or tight-binding method -- paved the way for the development of band theory.

In practice, this was a spectacularly complex procedure to perform rigorously. To re-express the Hamiltonian in terms of orbitals, one had to evaluate an enormous number of integrals of the form $\braket{\phi_n(\textbf{R}_i)|H|\phi_m(\textbf{R}_j)}$ -- one for each pair of orbitals separated by each lattice translation. Thus, finding the exact form of the Hamiltonian was in most cases an impossible task. Rather, the utility of this method became apparent when one accepted this impossibility and just guessed the form of $H$ \cite{slater_simplified_1954,harrison_tight-binding_1994}.\footnote{In \cite{slater_simplified_1954}, Slater and Koster provide a detailed account of the many complications involved with performing a rigorous LCAO calculation.

} 
With just a few assumptions one could simplify the Hamiltonian to the point that it only depended on a small number of parameters. For example, one would expect the coupling between two orbitals to vanish unless they were sufficiently close as to overlap. Furthermore, by studying the symmetries in a crystalline material, one finds many couplings that must be equivalent. Thus, the game became one of adjusting this small number of parameters until the tight-binding model matches the properties found in experiment.

This tight-binding picture is a radically different world for an electron to live in, in comparison to the original form of Schr\"odinger's Hamiltonian (Eqn.~\ref{eqn:schrodinger_H}). In Schr\"odinger's story, the electron exists as a wave in a fundamentally smooth world, where the potential defines a landscape through which the electron roams. However, in Bloch's story the collective properties of the electrons emerge from the overlap between a discrete lattice of orbitals. Here the landscape is more akin to an archipelago, with electrons that hop from island to island as in Fig.~\ref{fig:hopping_electron}. Constructing a model in this formalism is a completely different procedure to that of Schr\"odinger, since we must start by asking: \textit{What is the geometry of the lattice?} Only then can we decide the values of the couplings between lattice sites and build a Hamiltonian. Here, the geometry of the lattice has a fundamental role in deciding what kind of Hamiltonians we can build.

Of course, the tight binding method is just one of a huge variety of approximations that have been used to understand how electrons behave in materials. However, it is not the only context where the lattice geometry turns out to be absolutely central in determining what kinds of Hamiltonians can exist, and thus what kind of collective phases may emerge. In a crystalline material, the lattice structure determines the shape of the Brillouin zone, as well as the position of high symmetry points. Thus, the band structure that emerges is strongly constrained by the shape of the lattice \cite{simon_oxford_2013,nussbaum_crystal_1966}. In many magnetic materials, the role of lattice geometry arguably becomes even more important. Here the electron-electron repulsion is sufficiently strong that electrons become immobilised on each site. An effective description of the material treats each electron as completely fixed to a point on the lattice, and electrons interact only by realigning their spin and internal degrees of freedom in response to the state of a neighbour \cite{mott_basis_1949,roy_mott_2019}.

In condensed matter, the process of learning a new model can often feel similar to learning a board game: One starts by looking at the board, studying its symmetries and defects, and then progresses to learning the rules and finally playing the game. A natural question to ask is: \textit{How does the game depend on the shape of the board?} The equivalent question for a physicist is: \textit{How does the microscopic structure of a material determine the types of collective phases that can emerge?} For a given Hamiltonian, on a given lattice, we can ask how the phase changes when we alter the character of the lattice, for example adding in holes or defects, altering its connectivity, changing its symmetries or even completely disordering the lattice and destroying all spatial symmetries. In modelling a real system, we may also consider the effect of a disordered potential, applying an energy penalty to occupying certain regions over others. Over the last hundred years, this question has yielded an enormous variety of answers.   In some contexts, one finds that nothing really changes. Many properties in condensed matter are remarkably insensitive to the microscopic details -- that is, they are shared by an enormous variety of systems living on an enormous variety of lattices. Two famous examples of this phenomenon are that of universality, which we give a brief introduction to in \textsection\ref{sec:universality} and band topology, which we describe in \textsection\ref{sec:qhe}. On the other hand there are cases -- notably that of frustrated magnetism -- where the details of the lattice geometry are paramount in determining the properties of the system, which we shall discuss in \textsection\ref{sec:lattice_specific}.
