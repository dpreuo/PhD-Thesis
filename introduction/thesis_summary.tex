\section{Thesis Outline}

Thus far, we have introduced examples of two very different phenomena in condensed matter physics. In Section \ref{sec:universality}, we described the notion of universality, an inherently many-body property, that arises from the statistical averaging over enormous numbers of interacting particles. We also presented an introduction to the quantum Hall effect -- a manifestly non-interacting, single particle phenomenon. Both of these theories provided an answer to the question: \textit{How is it possible for many systems, with wildly different microscopic configurations, to realise the same types of collective properties?} Focussing on the quantum Hall effect, since it is most relevant to this thesis, the solution relied on the construction of a topological invariant, the Chern number, which could be calculated in any system with a notion of band structure. This provided us with a broad classification system, whereby vastly different Hamiltonians on a wide range of atomic lattices could be identified as sharing a `universal' set of properties determined by this invariant. The power of this approach was that it proved deeply indifferent to the microscopics of a system. Provided that a notion of band structure could be defined -- a relatively weak condition -- the Chern number allows us to make statements about the materials properties, such as its Hall conductivity and the presence of edge modes, without having to account for the details of a specific model.

On the other hand, in Section \ref{sec:lattice_specific} we told a very different story -- that of frustration in magnetic materials. Here we found that superficially similar materials, i.e.~those consisting of spins on a lattice, interacting according to a rather limited variety of couplings (Ferromagnetic, Antiferromagnetic, Ising, etc.), could give rise to an enormous variety of collective phases depending on the geometry of the material and the finer details of the spins' couplings. Often when frustration is present no single state can independently satisfy the requirement of every coupling, and the emergent phases are able to explore (and in the quantum case, entangle) a multitude of degenerate ground states. The character of these degenerate states, and the phases that emerge, are highly specific to the details of the system -- there is no universal consequence of frustration. In a classical, zero temperature context, the emergent phases can be extremely fragile, where the presence of even weak perturbations, including disorder, can serve to lift the delicate ground-state degeneracy and either allow a system to select a single preferred state, or result in glassy physics -- where system has many near-degenerate low-energy states which are well-separated in configuration space, and the order that forms depends unpredictably on quenched disorder \cite{edwards_theory_1975,binder_spin_1986}. In the quantum context, the situation is less precarious, since in general a gapped QSL state is robust against weak disorder, and many gapless QSL systems can also be shown to be robust \cite{savary_quantum_2017}. However, it remains the case that the formation of such materials generally depends on an intricate combination of lattice geometry and specific couplings. As we saw, depending on the lattice, the antiferromagnetic Heisenberg model can go from ordered (honeycomb/square) to VBS (star) to a potential QSL (kagome).

In this thesis, our line of inquiry will lie at the intersection of these two effects, where we investigate the veracity of both the assumptions presented above -- that is that Chern physics is inherently indifferent to microscopics, and that frustrated magnetism and the formation of spin liquids is inherently sensitive to it. In particular, we shall focus on the structural properties of a material, where our approach will frequently be to start with a known system, defined on a particular lattice, and then ask which properties are altered and which survive when we allow the lattice to change. The structure of this thesis is as follows:

Chapter \ref{chap:chern_intro} is largely introductory material, where we present a detailed explanation of the Chern number, describing how it may be calculated given the band structure of an arbitrary non-interacting Hamiltonian with translational symmetry. We consider both the case of a discrete and a continuous Brillouin zone. We finish this chapter by introducing a simple model for a Chern insulator, the Qi-Wu-Zhang model, and provide a straightforward generalisation to amorphous systems.

In Chapter \ref{chap:local_markers}, we focus on understanding how robust the Chern number is against the breaking of translational symmetry. As we discussed in Section.~\ref{sec:qhe}, the underlying physical phenomena -- the quantum Hall effect and topological insulators in general -- are interesting specifically \textit{because} they are robust against disorder. However, the Chern number is formulated as an integral over the Brillouin zone. Thus, in any physical system that lacks band structure, either through the presence of disorder or interactions, this quantity becomes ill-defined. Thus, there is a tension in our mathematical framework, where the tools we use to describe disorder-resistant phenomena are themselves fatally undermined by disorder. We introduce one of the methods that has been employed to circumvent this problem: the real-space Chern number. Several such proposals exist, of which we discuss three, namely the Chern marker \cite{bianco_mapping_2011}, the Bott index \cite{loring_guide_2019} and Kitaev's local marker \cite{kitaev_anyons_2006}, showing the ways that they may be considered equivalent to one another. Each of these markers was originally constructed with no clear connection to experiment or a simple physical interpretation of the quantity. We propose a physical observable -- a localised reformulation of a Hall measurement, showing that the measured conductivity corresponds to a local Chern marker when evaluated in the bulk of a Chern insulator. Thus, our conclusion is that there exists a formulation or Chern number, with a well-defined physical interpretation, that shares the disorder-indifferent nature of the underlying physics. 

In Chapter \ref{chap:amorphous_kitaev} we turn our attention back to frustrated magnetism, focussing on the Kitaev honeycomb model \cite{kitaev_anyons_2006}. We study the spin-$\frac 12$, quantum version of this system, providing a review of Kitaev's exact solution via a transformation to a Majorana fermion representation. Here we consider whether the Kitaev QSL is as fragile as one would expect, given the general discussion of frustrated magnetism presented above. To do this, we consider the possibility of constructing and solving a Kitaev Hamiltonian on a general disordered lattice, the only constraint being that it must remain tri-coordinated to allow for $x$, $y$ and $z$-bond labels to be assigned consistently. Remarkably, we find that an exact solution can be constructed on almost any tri-coordinated lattice. Furthermore, we verify that the presence of odd cycles on a general lattice leads to the formation of a ground state that spontaneously breaks time-reversal symmetry, transforming the originally gapless QSL phase into a gapped chiral amorphous QSL. In the construction of the exact solution, the strongly-interacting Kitaev model decomposes into a non-interacting system of Majorana fermions. As we shall see, the chiral gapped QSL phase depends explicitly on the Chern number of the Majorana Hamiltonian. Thus, we find that the robustness of this phase can be understood as a consequence of the robustness of the topological invariants presented in Chapter \ref{chap:local_markers}. In contrast to the general wisdom that `frustrated magnets are inherently fragile phases of matter', the Kitaev QSL can be constructed and solved on a wide variety of lattices, and even with the addition of strong bond-dependent disorder.

Finally, in Chapter \ref{chap:kitaev_star}, we consider the effect of adding geometric frustration to the Kitaev model. As discussed above, the frustration in this model stems from competition between the three bond types and, as we see in Chapter \ref{chap:amorphous_kitaev}, the pure Kitaev model is indifferent to the details of the lattice geometry. However, real candidate Kitaev materials -- such as the iridates Na$_2$IrO$_3$ and $\alpha$-Li$_2$IrO$_3$~\cite{singh_antiferromagnetic_2010, singh_relevance_2012,revelli_fingerprints_2020}, and $\alpha$-RuCl$_3$~\cite{sandilands_scattering_2015,banerjee_proximate_2016} -- do not realise a pure-Kitaev Hamiltonian. These materials are characterised by dominant Kitaev interactions, however they also contain other subdominant perturbations, such as Heisenberg couplings, which prevent the materials from realising Kitaev's QSL ground state. Previous studies of the Kitaev model that account for these extra terms have unearthed a rich phase diagram depending on the relative strength and sign of the perturbations. These generally contain QSL phases as well as a variety of ordered phases, for example Ne\'el and various stripe-ordered phases. As we have discussed, antiferromagnetic systems are often highly susceptible to geometric frustration. Thus, we ask whether constructing a `more realistic' Kitaev-Heisenberg (KH) model on a geometrically frustrated lattice can lead to the emergence of new magnetic order not seen on the conventional honeycomb model. The answer to this question is \emph{yes}. We construct the KH model on the star lattice --  the simplest example of a tri-coordinated lattice with odd cycles. Here, we find that, in the regions of the phase diagram where the Honeycomb model contains antiferromagnetic order, the star-lattice model forms two different valence bond (VBS) solid ground states. Thus, we find ourselves in the peculiar situation where of all the phases that emerge in our model, classically-ordered phases turn out to be the most susceptible to microscopic changes in the lattice, whereas the quantum spin liquid phases are among the most robust! Considering the effect of an applied magnetic field, we show that the VBS states hosts a rich phase diagram of topological triplon excitations and determine the Chern numbers of the bands.
